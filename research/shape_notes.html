The plots, one by one

Each plot has a Friday (laptop, 11 h) and a Saturday (VPS, 2 h 20 min) version. The notes read the Friday plot, which has more data; the grey line under each note says how Saturday differs.

1 · Spread in ticks

Skill 1 · liquiditySkill 3 · spread capture

How it is computed

For every clean second, take best ask − best bid and divide by the tick (1¢). Stack the share of seconds in each bin. Seconds where one side is empty are excluded (n = 111k s for 15m, 781k ladder, 193k Fed).

What to notice

  • 15m sits at exactly 1 tick 90 % of the time, and 97–99 % during the live minutes. The 9 % at 2 ticks is mostly the half hour before a window opens.
  • Ladder shows 80 % "<1 tick". That isn't tight markets: those are far strikes priced at 0.1–0.5¢, where the tick switches to 0.1¢.
  • Fed shows 60 % sub-tick for the same reason: three dead outcomes at 0.1¢ are pooled with two live ones that sit at 1 tick.

Vocabulary

Quoted spread = ask − bid. Effective spread = 2·|P − m| for an actual trade at P, mid m. Realized spread = 2·s·(P − mt+τ): what a maker actually keeps after the price has moved. Stage 3 measures the third.

Why a desk cares

A 1¢ tick on a 50¢ token is a 200 bp quoted spread. BTC on Binance quotes 0.01 on 76,000, about 0.001 bp. Polymarket is an extreme large-tick market. When the tick binds, nobody can quote tighter, so the spread says nothing about risk. Makers compete on queue priority, and edge has to show up in markouts, not in the quote.

Common mistake

Reading a pooled bar. The Fed and ladder bars mix live and dead instruments. Always split by instrument state (moneyness, live or dead, time to expiry) before drawing a conclusion. Plot 8 does that for the ladder.

What it leads to

15m and Fed: half-spread is fixed at 0.5¢, so profit = 0.5¢ − adverse selection per fill. Stage 3 markouts decide everything. Ladder near the money: spread is a real choice, so the fair-value model (stage 4) sets it.

Saturday: 15m live minutes 99–100 % at 1 tick; ladder 11 % at 1 tick (only far-dated strikes recorded).

2 · Depth within k ticks of mid

Skill 1 · depthSkill 2 · impact

How it is computed

Each second, add up shares × price on each side for levels within k ticks of the mid. Median across seconds, band = p25–p75, log scale. We store 5 levels, so k = 5 is a lower bound in deep books.

What to notice

  • 15m is a funnel: $62 at the touch, $462 within 3 ticks, about $1,000 within 5. Thin at the front, deep behind.
  • Ladder flattens after 3 ticks: there are few levels. Its band spans two orders of magnitude because near and far strikes are pooled.
  • Fed bid is flat at about $12k: nearly all of the depth sits at the touch. That is resting rewards inventory, not a staircase of orders.

Why a desk cares

The depth profile is the price-impact curve. It tells you how far a sweep travels, so it sets the cost of a toxic fill when you sit at level 1 vs level 2. It also tells you whether your quote is small next to the book (you are a price-taker on queue) or is the book (you set the price and carry the information risk).

Common mistake

Measuring depth in shares. The Fed bid has a median of 4.8 M shares but only $11.6k, because the shares sit on dead outcomes at 0.1¢. Convert to $, and for risk use p(1−p) per share, the variance of a binary payoff.

What it leads to

Sizing: a $100–200 quote on 15m is small next to $450 within 3 ticks but a large share of the $60 touch. Stage 5 (queue and fill model) and the simulator will use these curves to price sweeps.

Saturday: 15m thinner ($204 / $236 within 3 ticks); near-money ladder deeper ($2.9k vs $834).

3 · Queue at the best price vs trade size

Skill 2 · queue positionSkill 3 · toxicity

How it is computed

$ resting at the best bid (left bar) and best ask (right bar) each second: p25–p75 bar, median tick. The dashed line is the median trade message in $.

What to notice

  • 15m: median queue is about 10× the median trade, but p25 (about $17) is close to one trade. A median trade takes 15 % of the level it hits, and 26 % of trades wipe the level out.
  • Ladder: queue ≈ one trade. 42 % of trades wipe the level, so every fill here is a "big" fill relative to the book.
  • Fed: the queue is 60–250× a trade. A new order would wait days for a fill.

Why a desk cares

Your expected wait at the back of the queue is roughly

E[wait] ≈ (size ahead of you) / (taker $ per minute hitting your side + cancels ahead of you)

A small trade "nibbling" a deep queue is usually noise. A trade bigger than the whole level (a sweep) usually carries information. The nibble vs sweep mix is the toxicity question in its simplest form.

Common mistake

Modelling the queue as moving only through trades. On 15m, 80 % of added size is cancelled within 1 s, so you move up mostly because others cancel. And the orders that stay ahead of you may be stale. Queue models (Cont–de Larrard, Moallemi–Yuan) need a cancel rate, not just a trade rate.

What it leads to

Stage 3: markout conditional on "did the fill wipe the level?". Stage 5: fill probability by queue position, fitted on VPS data only (laptop timing was stalled).

Saturday: 15m touch $23 / $28; 28 % of trades wipe the level.

4 · Order flow rates

Skill 1 · orders & cancelsSkill 5 · latency

How it is computed

From the replay's labelled book changes: a size increase at a level is an add, a decrease not explained by a trade is a cancel. Trades come from the trade feed; taker orders are grouped messages. All per market-minute of clean time, log scale.

What to notice

  • 15m: 3,460 adds and 3,100 cancels against 30 trade messages per minute, an order-to-trade ratio of about 117. That average includes the quiet pre-window; the live minutes run about 10,000 adds per minute.
  • Ladder: about 1,650 adds per trade. Quotes are refreshed constantly and almost never traded.
  • Adds ≈ cancels everywhere: total resting size stays level while individual quotes are replaced.

Why a desk cares

The order-to-trade ratio is a standard venue health metric (European rules cap it for this reason). A high value means algorithmic makers re-pricing on a signal, here almost certainly Binance BTC. You are competing with bots that move their quotes within milliseconds of a BTC tick. Whoever is slowest is the stale quote that gets hit.

Common mistake

Treating these as order counts. Our data is L2 (size per price level, no order IDs), so an "add" can be several orders, a modify, or a cancel-replace. These are counts of level changes, a lower bound on messages.

What it leads to

The latency budget (feed + decide + send) matters more than code speed. Stage 3 checks whether fills that arrive right after a Binance move are the toxic ones. If so, a cancel-on-Binance rule is the first strategy component.

Saturday: about 5,300 adds per minute on 15m live minutes; 65 % of added size cancelled within 1 s (Friday 80 %).

5 · Taker order size

Skill 3 · adverse selectionSkill 1 · fills

How it is computed

Trade messages on the same token and side less than 50 ms apart form one taker order. $ = Σ size × price. Cumulative distribution, log x. n = 35,549 orders on 15m, 2,151 ladder, 822 Fed.

What to notice

  • A vertical cliff at $1 in every family. That looks like a venue minimum order size (to verify in the docs). Venue rules leave fingerprints in distributions; learn to spot them.
  • 15m is the leftmost curve: median $7, p99 $341. Small, retail-sized flow.
  • Ladder and Fed have fat right tails: p99 $2.9k and $7.5k. Rare large orders.
  • On 15m, 20 % of orders hit several makers and 7.5 % walk two or more levels, yet those sweeps carry 24.5 % of the volume.

Why a desk cares

Order size is the cheapest toxicity predictor. In most markets, larger orders are more informed and move the price more (the idea behind Kyle's λ and Easley–O'Hara). A maker who can tell a $7 nibble from a $300 sweep can keep the spread on the first and avoid the second.

Common mistake

Counting trade messages as orders. One taker order that hits five makers produces five messages, each with its own transaction hash. Counting messages makes orders look smaller and more numerous than they are, which hides the sweeps.

What it leads to

Stage 3's headline table: markout at 1 / 5 / 30 / 60 s, split by order-size bucket and by minute of window. If large orders carry most of term ② (adverse selection), quoting small size at the touch is the strategy.

Saturday: 15m $7 / $55 / $289; sweeps 31.8 % of volume.

6 · Jumps in the mid

Skill 3 · regimesSkill 5 · lead–lag

How it is computed

Left: |mid(t) − mid(t − 1 s)| ≥ 2, 5, 10 ticks. Right: the same over aligned 10-second blocks. Counts per hour of clean time, log y.

What to notice

  • 15m: 200 jumps per hour of ≥ 2 ticks within a second, 30 of ≥ 5, 4 of ≥ 10. The rate falls slowly with size, so big jumps are not rare.
  • The 10-s panel shows fewer 2-tick moves (about 70 per hour): several 1-s moves merge, and some revert within 10 s.
  • Ladder about 2 per hour, Fed 0.06 per hour (3 jumps in 54 h).

Illustration (not measured)

Say 1 in 10 of the 200 hourly 2-tick jumps hits our 100-share quote before we cancel. Each costs at least 1.5¢ per share beyond the half-tick we earn: 20 × 100 × $0.015 = $30 per hour of adverse selection. Our spread income has to beat that.

Why a desk cares

This plot gives the jump rate λ by size, the main input to the cancel rule from our toolkit:

cancel if λ · E[(|J| − h)⁺] · Q > ρ

Here h is your half-spread, Q your size, and ρ what you earn by staying. On a VPS sample, 18.6 % of 15m jumps came within 10 s of a ≥ 0.01 % Binance move, against a 2.2 % base rate (n = 70, provisional). Part of λ can be seen coming.

Common mistake

Treating every jump as a loss. A move that reverts (bounce) costs nothing, or even helps. A move that persists is what hurts. The gap between the 1-s and 10-s panels is the first hint of how much bounce there is, and the markout curve over τ measures it properly.

What it leads to

Stage 3 markouts at τ = 1–60 s separate bounce from persistent moves. Stage 4 fair value uses Binance to predict the persistent part, which is how a maker cancels before the jump rather than after.

Saturday: 15m 117 / 25 / 8 per hour at ≥ 2 / 5 / 10 ticks.

7 · The 15-minute window life cycle

Skill 3 · regimesSkill 5 · fair value

How it is computed

Every statistic above, grouped by minute 0–14 of each 15-minute market (45 windows on Friday). Minute 0 is the window start, the moment BTC's reference price is fixed.

What to notice

  • Minutes 0–9 are stable: 97–99 % at 1 tick, $1.5–1.9k within 3 ticks, 30–45 taker orders per minute.
  • From minute 10 it falls apart: depth goes to about $0 by minute 14, one side is empty 83 % of the time, jumps rise from 600 to 1,900 per hour, and taker orders peak at 100 per minute in minute 13.
  • |mid − 0.5| climbs from 0.06 to 0.37: by the end the outcome is mostly known.

The maths behind the collapse

P(Up) = Φ(z) with z = ln(S/S₀) / (σ√τ). Take a 5 bp BTC move at z = 0 and σ ≈ 26.5 bp per 15 min (volatility report):

τ = 15 min: σ√τ = 26.5 bp → Δz = 0.19 → ΔP = 7.5¢
τ = 1 min: σ√τ = 6.8 bp → Δz = 0.73 → ΔP = 26.8¢

The same BTC move is worth about 3.5× more ticks near expiry. That is the binary option's gamma blowing up. Makers withdraw because one Binance tick now moves fair value by many Polymarket ticks.

Why a desk cares

This is a regime change inside a single instrument, driven by the clock. Averaging any statistic over the whole window mixes two different markets. Practitioners segment by regime first: here, minute of window; elsewhere, time to an event, session open, or volatility state.

Common mistake

Trusting the last minute's numbers. Minute 14 has only 392 clean seconds from 18 tokens, because many windows are already one-sided or dead. It is a small, survivor-biased sample. Always show n per bucket; the report does.

What it leads to

Working hypothesis: quote minutes 0–9 only, pull at minute 10. Stage 3 tests it with markouts by minute. The volatility report agrees: σ in log-odds rises 8× from minute 0 to 14. The 30-minute pre-window (88 % at 1 tick, mid ≈ 0.50, very little flow) is a separate question: is anyone trading there?

Saturday: same shape. One side empty 72 % in minute 14, depth $1,366 → $0.

8 · The daily ladder by moneyness and time to expiry

Skill 5 · fair valueSkill 1 · liquidity

How it is computed

Moneyness = |strike / BTC − 1|, using a BTC price implied from the ladder itself on Friday (no Binance feed yet). Hours to 12:00 ET is the resolution time. Each bucket is time-weighted; the band is p25–p75.

What to notice

  • Near the money (< 1 %): at 1 tick only 29–43 % of the time and at ≥ 2 ticks 38–56 %, median 1.6–2.1 ticks. These are the only books in our batch where the spread is not stuck at the minimum.
  • Beyond 5 %: 2 % at 1 tick (97 % sub-cent), $55 of depth. Dead.
  • Depth peaks at strikes 1–2 % away ($3.3k), not at the money.
  • The time-to-expiry panels zig-zag (22 % at 1 tick at 3–6 h, 7 % at 12–24 h).

Why a desk cares

The ladder is a strip of digital options on BTC at 12:00 ET. Options desks don't quote in price; they quote in volatility, so that one quote rule works across strikes and maturities. A 2-tick spread at the money and a 2-tick spread on a 4 % strike are very different risks, and expressing the width in vol points (or in units of fair-value uncertainty) makes them comparable.

Common mistake

Reading the zig-zag as an effect of time. With one day of data, each time bucket holds different strikes (2–21 tokens each). The bucket at 12–24 h is mostly far strikes, which drags its 1-tick share down. That is a composition effect, a cousin of Simpson's paradox. The fix is a 2-D grid (moneyness × time) or a model, and more days.

What it leads to

Stage 4 fair value: P(ST > K) = Φ(d₂) per strike, using the volatility report's σ and checked for monotonicity across strikes (a higher strike can never be worth more; a violation is an arbitrage). Then we quote near-money strikes 3–48 h out at a width set by the model, not by the queue.

Saturday: only strikes ≥ 24 h from expiry were recorded; near-money ones were deeper ($2.9k within 3 ticks).

Five habits this stage teaches

Weight by time, not by events. Ask what the book looked like whenever you would have been there. Event-weighting over-counts busy minutes about 8×.
Segment by regime before you average. Minute of window, moneyness, time to expiry, live or dead. A pooled number can describe no real moment.
Dollars, not shares; orders, not messages. Convert to $, and to p(1−p) for risk. Group messages into taker orders before measuring size.
Know what your data can't see. L2 has no order IDs, so queue position, resting time and "adds" are inferred, and our numbers are bounds. State that next to the result.
Show n, mark provisional. At a desk, a number without its sample size and its regime is treated as if it doesn't exist.
